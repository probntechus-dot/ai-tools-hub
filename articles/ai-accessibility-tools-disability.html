AI Accessibility Tools for People with Disabilities

By Sarah Mitchell March 20, 2026 19 min read

A software engineer lost her hearing at 34 due to an autoimmune condition. Within weeks, she went from participating fully in team meetings to missing 70% of what was said. Lip reading was unreliable — especially with accents and masks. Colleagues wrote things down, but the pace of meetings left her perpetually behind. She tried AI-powered live captioning (Google's Live Transcribe and Otter.ai) and within days, she was back to full participation. The AI transcribed meetings in real-time with 95%+ accuracy, identified speakers, and generated summaries she could review later. A year in, she says the AI captions are actually better than hearing for meetings — she has a searchable written record of every conversation.

One billion people — 15% of the global population — live with some form of disability. For decades, assistive technology was expensive, clunky, and limited. AI has changed the equation dramatically. Tools that cost thousands now cost dollars. Capabilities that didn't exist (real-time sign language translation, AI-generated image descriptions for the blind, eye-tracking computer control) are now practical. The global AI accessibility market reached $4.7 billion in 2025 and is projected to hit $12.3 billion by 2028, driven by government mandates, corporate accessibility initiatives, and genuine technological breakthroughs.

AI Speech-to-Text and Communication Tools

Otter.ai: Real-Time Meeting Transcription ($0-$16.67/month)

Otter.ai has become the default AI transcription tool for people with hearing loss in professional settings. It transcribes speech in real-time with 95-97% accuracy for clear English speech, identifies individual speakers, and generates meeting summaries with action items.

Key AI capabilities:

  • Speaker identification: AI learns to distinguish between meeting participants, labeling transcriptions by speaker name after initial training (usually 2-3 meetings)
  • Technical vocabulary learning: The AI adapts to industry-specific terminology — medical, legal, technical jargon that generic transcription fails on
  • Real-time collaboration: Meeting participants can see the live transcription simultaneously, highlight important points, and add corrections
  • Zoom/Teams/Meet integration: Joins meetings automatically as a participant, generating transcriptions without any setup from the user
  • Search and organization: Every transcribed word is searchable — find exactly when someone said something across months of meetings

Limitations: Accuracy drops significantly with heavy accents (85-90%), background noise (80-85%), and multiple simultaneous speakers (75-80%). For users who need near-perfect accuracy, CART (Communication Access Realtime Translation) by human captioners remains more reliable at $120-$200/hour.

Google Live Transcribe (Free)

Google's built-in Android accessibility feature provides free, real-time speech-to-text in 80+ languages. For casual conversations, medical appointments, and daily interactions, it's remarkably capable — and it's free.

Key strengths:

  • Sound notifications: AI detects and alerts for non-speech sounds (doorbell, dog barking, smoke alarm, baby crying) — critical safety feature for deaf users
  • Multi-language support: 80+ languages with real-time switching — valuable for multilingual households or international settings
  • Offline mode: Basic transcription works without internet connection (reduced accuracy)
  • Vibration alerts: Vibrates when someone says your name or a configured keyword

AI-Powered Hearing Aids: Beyond Amplification

Modern AI hearing aids from Starkey, Phonak, and Oticon have evolved far beyond simple amplification. They're now cognitive assistants that process sound intelligently:

  • Scene classification: AI identifies the listening environment (quiet room, restaurant, car, outdoor) and adjusts processing in real-time — different algorithms for different acoustic challenges
  • Directional focus: AI determines which speaker the user is facing and enhances that voice while suppressing others — solving the "cocktail party problem" that traditional hearing aids fail at
  • Fall detection and health monitoring: Starkey hearing aids detect falls and automatically alert emergency contacts. Some models track brain health indicators, physical activity, and social engagement.
  • Bluetooth streaming with AI processing: Phone calls, music, and media stream directly through hearing aids with AI-optimized audio processing. Users report phone conversations are clearer through hearing aids than through normal earbuds.

Cost reality: AI hearing aids range from $1,500-$7,000 per pair. Over-the-counter options (Apple AirPods Pro with hearing aid mode, Sony CRE series) provide basic AI features for $200-$300. Insurance coverage varies dramatically — some cover 80%, others cover nothing.

Visual Accessibility: AI for Blind and Low-Vision Users

Be My Eyes + GPT-4 Vision: AI Visual Interpreter (Free)

Be My Eyes started as a volunteer service connecting blind users with sighted volunteers via video call. In 2023, they added "Virtual Volunteer" — GPT-4 Vision providing instant AI descriptions of anything the user's phone camera sees. The impact has been transformative.

Key AI capabilities:

  • Scene description: Point the camera at anything — a restaurant menu, a street scene, a product label — and get a detailed verbal description within seconds
  • Document reading: AI reads printed documents, handwritten notes, and even partially obscured text with high accuracy
  • Navigation assistance: Describes the physical environment including obstacles, doors, signage, and crowd density
  • Product identification: Identifies products, reads ingredient lists, checks expiration dates, and distinguishes between similar-looking items
  • Contextual understanding: Unlike simple OCR, GPT-4 understands context. Pointing at a restaurant menu, it doesn't just read text — it can describe the cuisine type, identify popular dishes, note allergen information, and explain unfamiliar items.

User story: A blind entrepreneur uses Be My Eyes to independently review physical mail, navigate new office spaces, identify clothing for meetings, and read whiteboards during in-person meetings. Tasks that previously required sighted assistance are now done independently in seconds.

JAWS and NVDA: AI-Enhanced Screen Readers

Screen readers — software that reads aloud everything on a computer screen — are the primary digital access tools for blind users. JAWS (Job Access With Speech, $1,395) and NVDA (NonVisual Desktop Access, free open-source) are the dominant platforms.

2025-2026 AI enhancements:

  • AI image descriptions: Screen readers now generate alt text for images that lack it — using AI vision models to describe what the image contains. This solves the chronic problem of unlabeled images on the web.
  • Intelligent page navigation: AI understands web page structure and offers navigation shortcuts — "skip to main content," "find the search bar," "go to the price" — even on poorly structured websites
  • Document summarization: AI summarizes long web pages, PDFs, and emails before reading the full content — saving time on content that may not be relevant
  • Dynamic content handling: AI detects and announces dynamic page changes (new messages, updated prices, loading content) that traditional screen readers often miss

Apple VoiceOver + AI: Built-In Visual Intelligence (Free)

Apple's VoiceOver screen reader, included free on every iPhone, iPad, and Mac, added significant AI capabilities in iOS 17-18:

  • Door detection: Uses LiDAR (on iPhone Pro models) to identify doors, read signs, and estimate distance — helping blind users navigate independently
  • People detection: Identifies how many people are nearby and their approximate distance — useful in social situations
  • Live image descriptions: AI describes photos, screenshots, and visual content in real-time, including facial expressions in photos of people
  • Point and speak: Point at physical objects (especially small text on appliances, thermostats, etc.) and get voice descriptions

Motor Accessibility: AI for Physical Disabilities

Tobii Dynavox: Eye-Tracking Computer Control ($1,500-$15,000)

For people with severe motor disabilities (ALS, spinal cord injuries, cerebral palsy), eye tracking provides computer access when hands and voice aren't options. Tobii Dynavox is the market leader, used by approximately 100,000 people worldwide including the late Stephen Hawking.

Key AI capabilities:

  • Gaze prediction: AI predicts where the user intends to look next based on eye movement patterns, reducing the precision needed for accurate selection — critical for users with involuntary eye movements
  • Dwell optimization: Learns the optimal dwell time (how long you look at a target to select it) for each user, adapting as fatigue increases throughout the day
  • Text prediction: AI predicts words and phrases based on conversation context, personal vocabulary, and communication patterns — enabling faster communication
  • Environmental control: Controls lights, TV, doors, and other smart home devices through eye gaze — providing independence for people who cannot physically interact with their environment
  • Social media and communication: AI-assisted email, messaging, and social media access at speeds approaching typical typing rates for experienced users

Cost and funding: Eye-tracking systems are expensive ($1,500-$15,000), but many are covered by insurance, Medicaid, or assistive technology programs. The Tobii Dynavox I-Series is classified as a medical device and qualifies for Medicare reimbursement in the US.

Apple Accessibility Suite: The Best Free Motor Accessibility (Free)

Apple has invested more in built-in accessibility than any other tech company, and their motor accessibility features are genuinely world-class:

  • Voice Control: Full device control by voice — including dictation, navigation, app launching, and custom commands. "Open Safari, go to Google, search for weather in New York, scroll down" — all hands-free.
  • Switch Control: Control iPhone/iPad/Mac with external switches (button presses, sip-and-puff, head movements) through scanning interfaces. Supports Bluetooth switches, adaptive buttons, and USB switches.
  • AssistiveTouch: Virtual on-screen buttons that replace physical button presses. Combined with head tracking (iPhone front camera), users can navigate by head movements alone.
  • Back Tap: Assign actions to double-tap or triple-tap on the back of the iPhone — useful for people who can tap but not perform complex gestures.

Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC)

AI-Powered AAC for Non-Speaking Individuals

Approximately 2 million Americans cannot use natural speech for daily communication. AAC (Augmentative and Alternative Communication) devices give them a voice — and AI has dramatically improved these tools:

  • Proloquo2Go ($250): iPad-based symbol communication app. AI predicts which symbols the user is likely to want based on conversation context, time of day, location, and past patterns. A user in a restaurant sees food-related symbols prominently; at school, educational vocabulary surfaces automatically.
  • TD Snap ($300): Tobii Dynavox's AAC platform with AI that learns individual communication patterns over months, becoming faster and more predictive over time. Integrates with eye tracking for users who can't use touch screens.
  • AI voice generation: Companies like Acapela and CereProc create personalized synthetic voices from recordings of the user's voice (captured before speech loss) or voice-banked samples. ALS patients who record their voice early in disease progression can continue "speaking" in their own voice through their AAC device indefinitely.
  • Brain-Computer Interface AAC: Experimental BCI systems (Synchron, Neuralink) are enabling locked-in patients to type by thought alone. While still early-stage, 2025-2026 results show typing speeds of 15-20 words per minute from thought-to-text — fast enough for basic conversation.

Cognitive Accessibility: AI for Learning Disabilities and Neurodivergence

Reading and Comprehension Support

  • Speechify ($0-$12/month): AI text-to-speech that reads any digital text aloud with natural-sounding voices. Particularly valuable for dyslexic users who comprehend better through listening than reading. Supports 20+ languages, adjustable speed, and highlighting of the currently spoken word.
  • Rewordify (Free): AI simplifies complex text to a user-specified reading level. Useful for people with cognitive disabilities, non-native speakers, or anyone struggling with dense documents. Hover over simplified words to see the original.
  • Immersive Reader (Free, built into Microsoft products): AI-powered reading tool that breaks words into syllables, highlights parts of speech, provides picture dictionaries, adjusts text spacing, and reads aloud — all configurable per user.
  • Natural Reader ($0-$10/month): OCR + AI text-to-speech that reads physical documents, PDFs, images of text, and web pages. Particularly useful for students with learning disabilities navigating printed textbooks.

Focus and Executive Function Support

  • Goblin Tools (Free): AI-powered task breakdown tool designed for ADHD and executive function challenges. Give it a task like "clean the kitchen" and it breaks it down into specific, manageable steps: "1. Clear dishes from the counter to the dishwasher. 2. Wipe down counters with spray cleaner. 3. Sweep the floor..." The specificity is key for people who freeze when facing open-ended tasks.
  • Tiimo ($0-$5/month): Visual daily planner with AI-suggested schedules for autistic people and those with ADHD. Uses visual timers, color-coded activities, and gentle transition warnings.
  • Brain.fm ($7/month): AI-generated focus music scientifically designed to improve attention. Uses neural phase locking — rhythmic audio patterns that synchronize brain activity. Studies show 12-15% improvement in sustained attention for ADHD users.

Sign Language AI: Breaking the Communication Barrier

Sign language translation AI is one of the most challenging — and promising — accessibility frontiers:

  • SignAll: Camera-based system that translates American Sign Language (ASL) to English text in real-time. Installed at select Starbucks stores, enabling deaf customers to order independently. Accuracy: 85-90% for common signs, dropping for complex or regional variations.
  • Hand Talk: Brazilian app translating Portuguese to Brazilian Sign Language (Libras) using an animated avatar. Over 5 million downloads, primarily used for education and basic communication.
  • Google's ASL recognition: Built into Google Meet for live ASL interpretation. The AI doesn't translate ASL to English — instead, it identifies when someone is signing and keeps their video prominently displayed. A subtler but practical accessibility improvement.

The translation challenge: Sign languages are not word-for-word translations of spoken languages. ASL has its own grammar, idioms, and spatial components that AI struggles to capture. Current translation AI works for basic communication but misses nuance. The deaf community has mixed feelings about translation AI — some welcome it, while others argue it devalues deaf culture and native sign language fluency.

Web Accessibility: AI Tools for Developers

Making websites accessible benefits the 15% of the population with disabilities and is legally required in many jurisdictions (ADA, WCAG 2.1, European Accessibility Act 2025):

  • accessiBe ($49-$799/month): AI-powered accessibility overlay that scans websites and applies fixes — alt text generation, keyboard navigation, color contrast adjustment, screen reader optimization. Controversial: disability advocates argue overlays are band-aids that don't fix underlying code problems, and some overlays actually interfere with screen readers.
  • axe by Deque ($0-$250/year): Developer-focused accessibility testing tool. AI identifies WCAG violations in code and suggests specific fixes. Unlike overlays, axe helps developers fix the actual code — the preferred approach.
  • WAVE (Free): Web accessibility evaluation tool that visualizes accessibility issues directly on the page. Not AI-powered but essential for testing.
  • UserWay ($49-$299/month): AI widget providing on-page accessibility adjustments (font size, contrast, spacing, cursor size). More transparent about limitations than some overlay competitors.

Developer recommendation: Use axe or WAVE for testing, fix issues in the actual code (not via overlays), test with real screen readers (NVDA is free), and involve disabled users in testing. Overlays are supplements, not solutions.

Funding and Cost Assistance

Assistive technology is often expensive, but multiple funding sources exist:

  • Medicare/Medicaid: Covers "medically necessary" assistive devices including eye-tracking systems, AAC devices, and AI hearing aids (with documentation)
  • State Assistive Technology Programs: Every US state has an AT program that provides device loans, demonstrations, and sometimes funding assistance
  • Vocational Rehabilitation: For working-age adults, VR agencies fund workplace accessibility tools including screen readers, speech-to-text software, and ergonomic adaptations
  • AbleData (abledata.acl.gov): Comprehensive database of assistive technology products with funding source information
  • Tech company accessibility programs: Apple, Google, and Microsoft provide significant accessibility features built into their products at no additional cost — often the best starting point

AI Accessibility in Education

Education is one of the most impactful domains for AI accessibility tools, transforming learning opportunities for students with disabilities:

Learning Disability Support

AI-powered reading assistants like Immersive Reader (built into Microsoft 365 and free) provide text-to-speech, syllable breaking, line focus, and picture dictionaries for students with dyslexia. The AI adapts reading level in real-time, simplifying complex text or providing definitions for challenging vocabulary. Studies show students with dyslexia using AI reading tools improved reading comprehension scores by 15-30% over a semester. Speechify and Natural Reader extend this to any digital content, converting textbooks, PDFs, and web pages into audiobook-quality narration with AI voices that maintain engagement better than robotic TTS.

Communication Aids for Non-Verbal Students

AI-powered Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC) devices have evolved dramatically. Proloquo2Go, TouchChat, and LAMP Words for Life use AI to predict the next word or phrase based on context, reducing the time needed to construct messages. Newer systems like AssistiveWare's Proloquo use machine learning to adapt vocabulary suggestions based on the user's communication patterns, time of day, and current activity. A non-verbal student in science class sees science-related vocabulary promoted; during lunch, social and food vocabulary surfaces. This contextual intelligence reduces communication effort by 40-60%.

ADHD and Executive Function Support

AI tools like Goblin.tools break complex tasks into manageable steps, while Structured and Routinery provide AI-optimized daily routine guidance. For students with ADHD, these tools address the core executive function challenges: task initiation, time estimation, prioritization, and task switching. AI learns individual patterns — if a student consistently underestimates homework time, the AI adjusts estimates and adds buffer time. Focus apps like Forest and Centered use AI to detect attention drift and provide gentle re-engagement prompts.

Workplace Accessibility: AI Accommodations That Scale

Remote Work AI Accessibility

The shift to remote work created both challenges and opportunities for workplace accessibility. AI-powered tools have largely solved the meeting accessibility gap: Zoom's AI Companion, Microsoft Teams Copilot, and Google Meet's AI provide real-time captioning with speaker identification, meeting summaries for people who need asynchronous access, and automatic action item extraction. For deaf and hard-of-hearing employees, these AI captions have reduced the need for human CART (Communication Access Real-time Translation) services — saving employers $150-$200 per meeting hour while providing comparable accuracy (95%+ for AI vs. 98%+ for human CART).

Document Accessibility Automation

Creating accessible documents (PDFs, presentations, reports) has historically been a manual, tedious process. AI tools now automate most of it. CommonLook and Equidox use AI to add proper heading structure, reading order, alt text for images, and table headers to existing PDFs — converting inaccessible documents to WCAG-compliant versions in minutes instead of hours. Microsoft's Accessibility Checker (built into Office) uses AI to flag accessibility issues in real-time as you create documents, preventing accessibility debt from accumulating.

Neurodiversity in the Workplace

AI tools are enabling employers to support neurodiverse employees (autism, ADHD, dyslexia) without requiring disclosure or special accommodation requests. Universal design AI — tools available to everyone — benefits neurodiverse employees disproportionately while avoiding stigma. Examples: AI meeting summarizers help employees with auditory processing difficulties; AI writing assistants help employees with dyslexia produce professional documents; AI schedule optimizers help employees with ADHD manage time and transitions. Companies like SAP, Microsoft, and JPMorgan have launched neurodiversity hiring programs that specifically leverage AI workplace tools.

The Legal Landscape: AI Accessibility Requirements

ADA and Section 508 Compliance

In the US, the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) increasingly applies to digital spaces. Recent DOJ guidance requires that websites and mobile apps of public accommodations be accessible to people with disabilities. Section 508 mandates accessibility for federal technology. AI tools help organizations meet these requirements: accessiBe, UserWay, and AudioEye use AI to automatically remediate common accessibility violations on websites — though automated overlays remain controversial in the accessibility community, with many advocates arguing they're insufficient substitutes for native accessible design.

European Accessibility Act (2025)

The European Accessibility Act, which took full effect in June 2025, requires accessibility for a broad range of products and services including computers, smartphones, e-commerce, banking, and transport services. The scope is broader than US requirements and affects any company operating in the EU market. AI accessibility testing tools like Deque's axe, WAVE, and Siteimprove are essential for compliance monitoring across large digital estates.

The Overlay Controversy

AI accessibility overlays (widgets that sit on top of websites claiming to make them accessible) are highly controversial. Proponents argue they provide immediate accessibility improvements at low cost. Critics — including many disability advocacy organizations — argue that overlays don't fix underlying accessibility problems, can actually break assistive technology compatibility, and give organizations a false sense of compliance. The National Federation of the Blind issued a statement opposing overlay products. Our recommendation: use AI for accessibility testing and native remediation, not overlay band-aids.

Emerging AI Accessibility Technologies

Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCI)

AI-powered brain-computer interfaces are advancing from research labs to early commercial products. Synchron (stentrode implant) and Neuralink have demonstrated paralyzed patients controlling computers through thought alone, with AI translating neural signals into cursor movements, text, and commands. Non-invasive BCI (headband-based) from companies like NextMind (acquired by Snap) and CTRL-Labs (acquired by Meta) use AI to interpret neural and muscular signals for device control. While still early, BCI promises to eventually provide computer access to people with the most severe physical disabilities.

Environmental Audio Intelligence

For deaf and hard-of-hearing individuals, AI now provides awareness of environmental sounds that hearing people take for granted. Sound Alert (Apple Watch), Oticon hearing aids, and Google's Sound Notifications use AI to identify specific sounds — doorbell, fire alarm, baby crying, dog barking, car horn — and deliver visual or haptic alerts. The AI distinguishes between sounds with 90-95% accuracy and can be customized to prioritize sounds most important to the individual user.

AI-Powered Prosthetics

Modern prosthetic limbs use AI to interpret muscle signals and adapt movement patterns. Ottobock's C-Leg uses AI to adjust knee resistance in real-time during walking, adapting to stairs, slopes, and different walking speeds automatically. Upper limb prosthetics from Open Bionics and TASKA Prosthetics use AI pattern recognition to translate muscle signals into precise hand movements — enabling grip patterns for different objects (cup, pen, handshake) without conscious effort. The AI learns each user's unique muscle signal patterns over weeks, improving control accuracy from 70% at initial fitting to 90%+ after adaptation.

Expanded FAQ

Are AI accessibility tools covered by insurance or government programs?

Medical-grade assistive devices (hearing aids, eye-tracking systems, AAC devices) are often covered by Medicare, Medicaid, or private insurance with a doctor's prescription and documentation of medical necessity. Software tools (screen readers, captioning apps, text-to-speech) are typically not insurance-covered but are often available free (NVDA, Google Live Transcribe, Apple VoiceOver) or funded through vocational rehabilitation programs for employment-related needs.

How accurate are AI-generated captions and alt text?

AI captioning (Otter.ai, Google) achieves 95-97% accuracy for clear English speech in quiet environments. Accuracy drops with accents (85-90%), background noise (80-85%), and technical terminology (75-85% without training). AI-generated alt text for images is approximately 85-90% accurate for common objects and scenes, but struggles with context, cultural references, and nuanced visual content. Human review improves AI-generated alt text significantly.

What accessibility tools do you recommend for someone newly disabled?

Start with the free built-in tools on your existing devices: Apple VoiceOver/Accessibility Suite (iPhone/Mac), Google Accessibility Suite (Android), Windows Accessibility features. These cover basic needs immediately at zero cost. Then assess specific needs: hearing loss → Google Live Transcribe + Otter.ai; vision loss → Be My Eyes + NVDA/VoiceOver; motor disability → Apple Switch Control/Voice Control; cognitive → Speechify + Immersive Reader. Connect with your state's Assistive Technology program for device loans to try before buying.

Can AI translate sign language in real-time?

Partially. SignAll and similar systems translate common ASL signs to English text with 85-90% accuracy in controlled settings. Real-world accuracy is lower due to: signing speed variation, regional sign dialects, the spatial grammar of sign languages that AI struggles to parse, and the importance of facial expressions in sign language (which current AI often ignores). The technology is useful for basic communication but not yet reliable enough for complex or legal conversations.

Are accessibility overlays (accessiBe, UserWay) effective?

Controversial. Overlay companies claim to make websites WCAG-compliant with one line of code. Disability advocates and accessibility professionals argue that overlays: often interfere with existing assistive technology (especially screen readers), create a false sense of compliance without fixing underlying code, and don't address the most critical accessibility barriers. The consensus among accessibility experts: overlays are better than nothing but significantly worse than proper code-level accessibility fixes. Use them as a temporary measure while investing in genuine accessibility improvements.

What's the best free accessibility technology?

Apple's built-in accessibility suite (VoiceOver, Switch Control, Voice Control, AssistiveTouch, Live Captions) is arguably the most comprehensive free accessibility platform in existence. Google's suite (TalkBack, Live Transcribe, Lookout, Sound Notifications) is the best on Android. NVDA is the best free screen reader for Windows. Be My Eyes provides free AI visual assistance. Microsoft's Immersive Reader is free across Office products. You can build a remarkably capable assistive technology setup using only free tools.

How is AI changing employment for people with disabilities?

Significantly. AI tools are eliminating many workplace barriers: real-time captioning enables deaf participation in meetings, AI screen readers make digital workplaces accessible to blind employees, voice control enables motor-disabled workers to use computers, and cognitive tools support neurodiverse employees. Remote work (normalized post-pandemic) removes physical accessibility barriers entirely. The disability employment gap is narrowing — though it remains significant, AI tools are a major contributing factor to the improvement.

The Bottom Line: AI Accessibility in 2026

AI accessibility tools have crossed a threshold where they genuinely transform daily life for people with disabilities. The combination of free built-in platform features (Apple, Google, Microsoft), affordable AI apps (Otter.ai, Be My Eyes, Speechify), and increasingly capable specialized devices (AI hearing aids, eye trackers, AAC tools) means that more barriers are removable than ever before.

Our recommendations:

  • Hearing loss: Start with Google Live Transcribe (free), upgrade to Otter.ai for work meetings, explore AI hearing aids for all-day use
  • Vision loss: Apple VoiceOver or NVDA for screen reading, Be My Eyes for visual assistance, explore smart canes and wearable AI (Envision Glasses)
  • Motor disability: Apple Accessibility Suite first (free, remarkably capable), then Tobii Dynavox for eye tracking if needed
  • Cognitive/learning: Speechify for reading, Goblin Tools for task management, Immersive Reader for document accessibility
  • Communication: Proloquo2Go or TD Snap for AAC, investigate voice banking early if progressive condition
  • Web developers: axe by Deque for testing, fix code directly, test with real screen readers, involve disabled users

The disability rights movement's rallying cry — "Nothing About Us Without Us" — applies to technology too. The best AI accessibility tools are built with disabled users as co-designers, not afterthoughts. When evaluating tools, prioritize those with active disability community involvement in their development and testing.
