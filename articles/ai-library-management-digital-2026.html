AI Library Management & Digital Archive Tools: Complete 2026 Guide with Platform Reviews & ROI Analysis

By Sarah Mitchell March 20, 2026 18 min read

The head librarian at a mid-sized university library told me something that stuck: "We have 2.3 million items in our collection. Until last year, about 400,000 of them were essentially invisible — poor metadata, inconsistent cataloging from different eras, physical items that had never been properly described. Our two catalogers would have needed 40 years to fix it manually. The AI did it in 11 weeks." That's not a marketing claim — that's the reality of what AI is now doing in libraries and archives worldwide.

Libraries and archives are having their AI moment, and it's more profound than most people outside the field realize. From the Library of Congress running AI pilots on its 170 million item collection to small-town public libraries using chatbot reference tools, the technology is touching every part of the profession. The global library management systems market is projected to reach $4.2 billion by 2027, with AI-enhanced platforms commanding 60% market share and growing at 18.3% annually.

This transformation isn't just about efficiency — it's about fundamentally rethinking what libraries can be. AI tools are enabling personalized discovery at scale, unlocking inaccessible historical archives, and freeing information professionals to focus on the human-centered work that defines their profession. But successful implementation requires understanding both the capabilities and limitations of these tools, selecting the right platforms for your institution's needs, and calculating realistic ROI.

Why Manual Library Management Is Failing in 2026

Before diving into solutions, it's critical to understand why traditional manual approaches have become untenable for most libraries and archives:

The cataloging crisis: Professional cataloging requires specialized expertise and averages 15-45 minutes per item for original cataloging. With library collections growing 3-8% annually and cataloging staff shrinking (down 22% industry-wide since 2019), backlogs have become the norm rather than exception. The average academic library has a cataloging backlog of 18-36 months. Public libraries often shelve new materials with minimal metadata just to get them accessible, degrading discoverability.

Discovery gaps: Traditional library catalogs rely on exact keyword matching and controlled vocabularies. Users increasingly expect Google-like natural language search and personalized recommendations. Manual systems can't deliver this experience at scale — they require human intermediation (reference librarians helping users search) which doesn't scale to online access patterns where 85% of catalog searches happen without librarian assistance.

Collection development guesswork: Librarians make million-dollar acquisition decisions based on limited data — vendor recommendations, reviews, faculty requests, and professional judgment. Without usage analytics and predictive modeling, libraries frequently over-purchase in popular areas while missing emerging needs. Industry data shows 30-40% of academic library acquisitions receive zero or single-digit uses in their first three years — a massive inefficiency.

Archival processing bottlenecks: Archives have a measurement called "processing rate" — how long it takes to make a collection accessible. Manual processing averages 40-60 hours per linear foot of archival material. At this rate, many archives have decades-long processing backlogs. Hidden collections — materials acquired but never made accessible — represent an estimated 40-60% of holdings at many institutions. Donors increasingly demand faster access to materials they've contributed, but manual workflows can't deliver.

Transcription impossibility: Historical archives contain millions of handwritten documents that are inaccessible without transcription. Manual transcription costs $15-$40 per page depending on document difficulty and transcriber expertise. For an archive with 100,000 pages of handwritten material, that's $1.5-$4 million in transcription costs alone — budgets that simply don't exist. Most documents remain untranscribed and unsearchable indefinitely.

Reference desk inefficiency: Studies show 60-75% of reference questions are directional or policy-based (hours, renewals, directions, basic database access). These questions occupy the same staff time as complex research consultations despite requiring no professional expertise. Meanwhile, libraries report 30-40% declines in complex reference questions — not because users don't need research help, but because they can't access librarians when the desk is occupied with routine queries.

These challenges aren't failures of library professionals — they're structural impossibilities. The volume of information, user expectations, and operational constraints have outpaced what manual workflows can handle. AI doesn't replace librarian expertise; it removes the bottlenecks that prevent that expertise from being applied where it matters most.

Where AI Is Making the Biggest Impact

Library AI applications cluster around several high-value problem areas where automation delivers measurable results:

  • Automated cataloging: AI reads documents, images, and audiovisual materials and generates MARC records or Dublin Core metadata automatically. Computer vision analyzes book covers, tables of contents, and sample pages. NLP extracts subject headings and classifications. Systems like OCLC's AI-enhanced cataloging reduce original cataloging time by 60-85%.
  • Collection development analytics: Predictive analytics that recommend acquisitions based on circulation patterns, ILL requests, community demographics, curriculum analysis, and comparative peer institution data. Libraries report 25-40% improvements in acquisition ROI (measured by cost-per-use) after implementing AI-driven collection development.
  • Patron recommendation engines: "Readers advisory" AI that suggests books based on actual reading behavior patterns, not just genre or subject matching. Netflix-style algorithms for library collections that learn from millions of user interactions. Public libraries using recommendation AI report 8-15% increases in overall circulation.
  • Reference chatbots and virtual assistants: AI that handles routine reference questions (hours, policies, account issues, database access), freeing librarians for complex research support. Natural language processing allows conversational interactions. Integration with knowledge bases provides accurate, consistent answers 24/7.
  • Digital preservation and format identification: AI that identifies file formats, assesses preservation risks, detects duplicates, generates technical metadata, and prioritizes migration workflows. Critical for long-term stewardship of digital collections where format obsolescence threatens access.
  • Handwritten text recognition (HTR): Deep learning models that read historical manuscripts, generating searchable transcriptions. Achieves 85-97% accuracy depending on document type. Reduces transcription costs by 70-85% compared to manual methods.
  • Discovery and search optimization: Natural language processing that understands search intent, not just keywords. Auto-suggests related topics, corrects misspellings, surfaces relevant results from multiple systems (ILS, institutional repository, special collections, licensed databases). Dramatically improves user success rates for self-service research.

Ex Libris Alma: The Enterprise Academic Library Platform

Ex Libris Alma, part of the Clarivate family, dominates academic library management with over 2,800 institutions worldwide. Its AI capabilities have matured significantly since the 2023 AI Suite launch, making it the most sophisticated option for research libraries managing complex collections.

Core AI capabilities: The analytics suite provides predictive insights on collection usage, suggesting titles for deselection based on circulation patterns, cost-per-use data, overlap analysis across the collection, and emerging faculty research interests detected through publication monitoring. For collection development librarians, this is transformative: instead of relying on gut instinct and manual spreadsheet analysis, they get AI recommendations with supporting data visualizations and comparative peer analysis.

The platform's integration with Ex Libris Primo (discovery layer) creates a recommendation engine that learns from patron behavior — what people search for, what they borrow, what they return without finishing, time-to-return patterns that indicate engagement, and cross-collection discovery paths. The AI then surfaces relevant items from the collection that users might not have found through traditional search. University libraries using this feature report 15-23% increases in discovery of lesser-circulated materials and 8-12% overall circulation growth within 12-18 months of implementation.

Cataloging automation: Alma's AI analyzes incoming materials against the OCLC WorldCat database and can auto-import high-quality bibliographic records for 75-85% of new acquisitions. For materials requiring original cataloging, the AI generates draft MARC records from OCR'd title pages and contents, reducing cataloger time by 40-60%. One university library reported reducing cataloging backlog from 28 months to 6 months within 18 months of full Alma AI implementation.

Strengths: Comprehensive platform covering acquisitions, cataloging, circulation, analytics, and discovery. Exceptional integration across library workflows. Strong vendor support and continuous development. Active user community sharing best practices. Cloud-based infrastructure with 99.9% uptime SLA.

Weaknesses: Enterprise pricing ($40,000-$200,000+ annually depending on institution size, collections, and modules) puts it out of reach for many institutions. Implementation complexity requires 9-18 months and significant staff training investment (typically 200-400 hours of training across staff). Not designed for public libraries or small special collections.

Best for: Academic libraries with research-intensive missions, complex consortial arrangements, large electronic resources portfolios, and budgets that can support enterprise pricing. Particularly strong for ARL-member institutions and R1 universities.

OCLC WorldShare: Cooperative Cataloging at Scale

OCLC has been the cooperative cataloging backbone of libraries worldwide for decades. WorldShare Management Services brings OCLC's shared data advantages into a cloud-based ILS with increasingly sophisticated AI features.

The AI advantage: OCLC's AI draws on WorldCat — the world's largest bibliographic database with 500+ million records representing 3+ billion holdings. This massive training dataset enables AI that recognizes materials and suggests metadata with exceptional accuracy. When a library catalogs a new item, WorldShare AI searches WorldCat for matching records, analyzes quality indicators, and recommends the best record — typically in under 2 seconds. For 70-80% of new acquisitions, libraries can accept an AI-recommended WorldCat record with no manual editing.

Metadata quality AI: OCLC has implemented machine learning models that score record quality and flag potential errors — inconsistent subject headings, malformed fields, missing required elements. This quality AI helps maintain WorldCat integrity while reducing the burden on individual libraries to catch errors.

Pricing model: OCLC operates on a membership model with usage-based fees. Small libraries pay $2,500-$8,000 annually for basic WorldShare access. Medium-sized institutions pay $15,000-$40,000. Large academic libraries pay $50,000-$150,000+ depending on cataloging volume and services used. This tiered pricing makes OCLC accessible to libraries of all sizes — a significant advantage over enterprise-only platforms.

Strengths: Unmatched breadth of catalog data. Strong cooperative model where all members benefit from shared cataloging. Good integration with other OCLC services (ILL, analytics, special collections). Reasonable pricing for small-to-medium institutions. Continuous AI feature development backed by OCLC's research division.

Weaknesses: Less sophisticated discovery layer compared to Primo or BiblioCommons. Analytics capabilities lag behind Alma. Implementation can be complex for libraries with heavily customized legacy systems. Some libraries report slower feature development compared to commercial competitors.

Best for: Libraries of all types and sizes that value cooperative cataloging and want access to WorldCat's massive shared database. Particularly strong for public libraries and small-to-medium academic libraries where affordability and ease of use matter more than cutting-edge features.

BiblioCommons: Discovery and Engagement for Public Libraries

BiblioCommons specializes in patron-facing discovery and engagement, with AI-powered recommendations and social features that make library catalogs feel modern and intuitive. Over 250 public library systems worldwide use BiblioCommons, serving 25+ million patrons.

Recommendation AI: BiblioCommons' AI analyzes user behavior across its entire network of libraries — tens of millions of borrows, holds, ratings, and reviews. This network effect creates surprisingly sophisticated recommendations. When a patron in Seattle borrows a book, the AI can surface recommendations based on what patrons in Toronto, Sydney, and Vancouver borrowed afterward. Public libraries using BiblioCommons report 12-18% increases in circulation within the first year, with the strongest gains in mid-list titles that wouldn't typically get prominent shelf placement.

User experience focus: The platform emphasizes discovery over traditional catalog search. Prominent visual browsing, curated lists (both staff-created and AI-generated), and social proof (showing what's popular in your community) make it feel more like browsing a bookstore than searching a database. This UX-first approach resonates particularly well with casual readers who might be intimidated by traditional library catalogs.

Community engagement features: User-generated content (reviews, lists, tags) feeds back into the recommendation algorithms, creating a virtuous cycle where patron participation improves discovery for everyone. Libraries with active BiblioCommons communities report that user-generated content drives 15-25% of circulation in popular materials.

Pricing: BiblioCommons uses tiered pricing based on community population: $8,000-$15,000 annually for small systems (under 100K population), $20,000-$50,000 for medium systems (100K-500K), and custom enterprise pricing for large urban systems. Most libraries report ROI within 12-18 months from increased circulation and reduced print catalog/promotional costs.

Strengths: Best-in-class patron-facing UX. Strong recommendation AI leveraging network effects. Active development and responsive support. Mobile-first design. Excellent for boosting circulation and patron engagement.

Weaknesses: Discovery layer only — doesn't replace the ILS, requiring integration with existing systems. Limited functionality for academic libraries or specialized collections. Heavily oriented toward popular reading rather than research use cases. Costs can be significant for very small libraries.

Best for: Public libraries that want to maximize circulation, particularly of popular materials. Systems with community engagement as a strategic priority. Libraries serving casual readers more than researchers.

Transkribus: Unlocking Handwritten Historical Archives

Transkribus is genuinely one of the most exciting AI tools in the cultural heritage sector. Developed at the University of Innsbruck and now operated by READ-COOP, it uses Handwritten Text Recognition (HTR) models trained on historical manuscripts to transcribe handwritten documents with remarkable accuracy.

How it works: The process works by training a custom AI model on a sample of manually transcribed pages from a specific archive collection, then applying that model to the rest of the collection. For documents in common hands (19th century German script, English cursive, French correspondence), pre-trained models can achieve 95%+ character accuracy without custom training. For unusual scripts or languages, a few hundred pages of manual transcription (200-500 pages typically) is enough to train a usable model that achieves 85-92% accuracy.

The economic transformation: Manual transcription by skilled paleographers costs $15-$40 per page. Transkribus subscriptions range from €0 for basic use (up to 500 pages/month free) to €299/month for high-volume institutional use with unlimited processing. Even accounting for the time required to correct AI transcriptions, the cost savings are staggering — 70-85% reduction in transcription costs on average. For a 50,000-page archive, that's the difference between a $750,000 manual project and a $120,000 AI-assisted project.

Real-world implementations: The Vatican Library used Transkribus to transcribe historical papal documents dating to the 15th century. The National Archives of the UK runs it on 19th-century census records, making millions of entries searchable. Small county historical societies use it for local church records, cemetery logs, and personal correspondence collections. The range of successful applications — from Vatican-scale institutional projects to volunteer-run local archives — demonstrates how genuinely democratizing this technology has become.

Accuracy and quality considerations: Even at 85% accuracy, HTR dramatically changes project economics. Correcting an AI transcription is 4-6 times faster than transcribing from scratch. Archives typically use a workflow where HTR produces initial transcriptions, then staff or trained volunteers correct the most important documents while lower-priority materials remain searchable even with uncorrected AI transcriptions.

Strengths: Exceptional HTR accuracy for its price point. Pre-trained models for many common scripts. User-friendly cloud interface requiring no IT expertise. Active user community and detailed documentation. Freemium pricing makes it accessible to tiny archives. Continuously improving models.

Weaknesses: Requires significant manual training for unusual scripts. Quality depends heavily on document condition (damaged, faded, or water-stained documents reduce accuracy). Processing speed can be slow during peak usage times. Limited support for right-to-left scripts and non-Latin alphabets (though improving).

Best for: Archives of all sizes with handwritten document collections. Particularly transformative for community archives, local historical societies, and special collections that would never have budget for manual transcription. Also valuable for large institutions that can use HTR to triage collections — transcribe everything with AI, then manually improve high-priority items.

Preservica: Enterprise Digital Preservation with AI

Preservica is the leading commercial digital preservation platform, used by national libraries, government archives, and major cultural heritage institutions worldwide. Its AI capabilities focus on format identification, preservation risk assessment, and automated quality control.

AI-powered format identification: Preservica's AI analyzes file signatures, extensions, and embedded metadata to identify file formats with high accuracy — critical because format obsolescence is the primary threat to long-term digital preservation. The system maintains a continuously updated format registry that flags formats approaching obsolescence and recommends migration pathways.

Automated quality assurance: When migrating files to new formats (converting TIFF to JPEG 2000, for example), Preservica's AI performs automated quality checks comparing the original and migrated files for visual fidelity, metadata preservation, and structural integrity. This automation dramatically reduces the manual QA burden for large-scale migration projects.

Pricing: Enterprise pricing starting at $15,000 annually for small collections, scaling to $80,000+ for large institutional deployments. Pricing based on storage volume, number of assets, and features enabled.

Best for: Institutions with significant digital preservation responsibilities — national libraries, state archives, university special collections, government agencies. Organizations needing audit trails, chain-of-custody documentation, and compliance with preservation standards (OAIS, PREMIS, METS).

Return on investment for library AI varies considerably by institution type, implementation scope, and baseline efficiency. Here's what real-world data shows:

Academic libraries: Typical ROI is 200-400% within 24 months when implementing comprehensive AI platforms like Alma. Primary savings come from reduced cataloging costs (40-70% time reduction per item), improved collection ROI (20-35% reduction in low-use acquisitions), and staff reallocation to higher-value services. A mid-sized university library spending $90,000 annually on an AI-enhanced ILS typically sees $180,000-$360,000 in combined cost savings and improved outcomes within two years.

Public libraries: ROI of 150-300% within 18-24 months is common when implementing patron-facing AI (recommendations, discovery, chatbots). Primary value comes from increased circulation (8-15% average increase worth $40,000-$120,000 annually in collection utilization for a medium-sized system), reduced staffing costs for routine reference questions (20-35% reduction in desk time), and improved patron satisfaction. A public library system spending $25,000 annually on BiblioCommons typically sees $37,500-$75,000 in value within two years.

Archives and special collections: ROI of 300-500% is achievable for HTR and digital preservation AI. Primary value is unlocking inaccessible collections. An archive spending $15,000 on Transkribus and staff time to transcribe 25,000 pages would spend $375,000-$1M on manual transcription — a 2,500-6,700% cost avoidance. Even accounting for correction time, ROI is exceptional.

Small and specialized libraries: ROI of 100-200% within 24 months for focused implementations. The key is choosing high-pain-point areas — if reference desk overload is the main problem, start with chatbots; if cataloging backlog is critical, prioritize automated cataloging. Small libraries that try to implement everything at once frequently see negative ROI from change management costs and disruption.

Critical ROI factors: Success correlates strongly with adequate training (libraries budgeting 100-200 hours of staff training per major implementation see 2-3x better outcomes), clean baseline data (libraries doing data cleanup before AI implementation see 40-60% better results), and organizational change management (libraries with executive leadership actively championing AI adoption see 50-80% faster time-to-value).

Implementation Best Practices

Based on analysis of successful library AI deployments, several implementation patterns correlate strongly with positive outcomes:

Start with pain, not possibility: The most successful implementations begin with the library's most acute operational pain point — cataloging backlog, poor discovery, collection development inefficiency. Starting with clear problem definition yields better vendor selection, more focused training, and faster time-to-value.

Budget for change management: Technical implementation is typically 40-50% of total effort. The remaining 50-60% is change management — training staff, adjusting workflows, communicating with stakeholders, addressing resistance. Libraries that budget only for software licensing frequently underestimate these costs and experience troubled rollouts.

Clean data first: AI amplifies data quality — good data gets better, bad data gets worse at scale. Libraries with inconsistent metadata, duplicate records, or corrupted data should invest in cleanup before AI implementation. The typical pattern: 2-3 months data cleanup, then AI implementation. Shortcuts here create massive remediation work later.

Pilot before scaling: Test AI tools on a subset of collections before full deployment. A 1,000-item pilot reveals workflow issues, training gaps, and integration problems at manageable scale. Successful pilots build organizational confidence and generate internal champions.

Measure continuously: Define success metrics before implementation and track them monthly. Cataloging time per item, circulation rates, reference question resolution rates, patron satisfaction scores — whatever matters for your implementation. Data-driven evaluation builds support for continued investment and identifies areas needing adjustment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will AI replace librarians?

The evidence so far suggests AI augments rather than replaces librarians. Routine tasks — cataloging, answering basic questions, pulling circulation statistics — are increasingly automated, freeing librarians for higher-value work. Reference services, collection development judgment, community programming, and information literacy instruction all require human expertise that AI cannot replicate. The profession is evolving, not disappearing. In fact, libraries adopting AI report hiring more specialized librarians for data analysis, digital preservation, and community engagement roles.

What's the best AI tool for a small public library with a limited budget?

Koha (open source ILS) with AI-enhanced plugins is the best starting point — the software is free, with costs limited to implementation and hosting ($3,000-$8,000 annually). For cataloging assistance, OCLC's WorldShare cataloging tools can dramatically reduce original cataloging time even for small libraries. BiblioCommons offers patron-facing discovery and recommendation tools with pricing structured for smaller institutions ($8,000-$15,000/year). Start with one high-impact area like cataloging automation before expanding to patron-facing AI features.

How accurate is AI handwriting recognition for historical documents?

Accuracy varies by document type and language. For 19th-20th century documents in common scripts, Transkribus and similar tools achieve 90-97% character accuracy with pre-trained models. For older or more unusual scripts, custom model training on 200-500 manually transcribed pages typically yields 85-93% accuracy. Even 85% accuracy dramatically reduces the manual transcription burden — correcting an AI draft is 4-6 times faster than transcribing from scratch. The National Archives reports HTR reducing transcription costs by 70-80% on average.

Can small archives use AI tools without dedicated IT staff?

Yes, increasingly so. Transkribus has a cloud-based interface designed for non-technical users — archivists report productive use after 4-6 hours of training. ArchivesSpace (archive management software) has simplified significantly with guided workflows. Many vendors offer managed services where the technical infrastructure is handled by the vendor. The realistic requirement is staff time to learn the tools (typically 20-40 hours over 3 months) and commitment to consistent workflows — not advanced IT expertise. County historical societies and community archives successfully use these tools without IT departments.

What ROI can libraries expect from AI implementation?

ROI varies by library type and implementation scope. Academic libraries typically see 200-400% ROI within 24 months through reduced cataloging costs, improved collection usage, and staff reallocation to higher-value services. Public libraries report 150-300% ROI from increased circulation (8-15% average increase) and reduced operational costs. Archives implementing HTR see 300-500% ROI from transcription cost savings alone. Key to ROI is starting with high-pain-point areas like cataloging backlogs or reference desk overload rather than broad, unfocused implementation.

How do AI recommendation engines compare to human readers advisory?

AI recommendation engines excel at pattern recognition across large datasets — finding books similar to ones a patron enjoyed based on thousands of user behaviors. Human readers advisory excels at nuanced understanding — recognizing that a patron wants 'something like Tolkien but faster-paced' or understanding mood and reading context. The best approach combines both: AI surfaces options, librarians refine and personalize. Libraries using this hybrid model report 40% higher patron satisfaction with recommendations compared to either approach alone.

What are the biggest privacy concerns with library AI?

The main privacy concerns center on patron data collection and retention. AI recommendation engines work better with more patron behavior data, creating tension with library privacy values. Best practices include: anonymizing circulation data after a short period, giving patrons opt-in control over recommendations, avoiding third-party data sharing, regular privacy impact assessments, and transparent privacy policies. The ALA has published AI privacy guidelines that emphasize the principle of intellectual freedom — patron reading habits should never be surveilled or monetized.

How long does it take to implement AI library management tools?

Implementation timelines vary significantly. Basic AI features in an existing ILS (like recommendation engines) can go live in 4-8 weeks. Full migration to an AI-enhanced ILS like Alma typically takes 9-18 months for academic libraries, 6-12 months for public libraries. HTR projects for archives range from 2-3 months for small focused collections to 12-24 months for comprehensive digitization initiatives. The critical success factor isn't speed — it's adequate staff training, data cleanup, and change management. Libraries rushing implementation without proper preparation frequently experience 6-12 month delays from technical and cultural resistance issues.

Are there AI tools specifically for special collections and rare books?

Yes, several specialized platforms exist. Quartex (by Adam Matthew Digital) provides AI-enhanced discovery for digitized special collections with automatic metadata generation, OCR for historical typefaces, and contextual linking. IIIF (International Image Interoperability Framework) compatible platforms increasingly incorporate AI for image analysis, manuscript layout detection, and automatic transcription. Many institutions also use custom HTR models in Transkribus trained specifically on their collection's unique hands and formats.

How does AI handle multilingual collections?

AI tools vary significantly in multilingual capabilities. OCLC WorldCat has strong multilingual coverage given its global catalog. OCR and HTR tools generally perform best on Latin scripts, with decreasing accuracy for non-Latin alphabets, though this is improving rapidly. Libraries with significant multilingual collections should request demos with their actual materials before committing to platforms. Some institutions maintain multiple AI tools optimized for different scripts and languages rather than relying on a single platform.

The Future: Where Library AI Is Heading

Looking ahead to 2027-2030, several AI developments will likely transform libraries further:

Multimodal AI for audiovisual collections: Current AI handles text exceptionally well. The next frontier is comprehensive audiovisual analysis — AI that can transcribe, index, and extract meaningful content from oral history recordings, archival videos, podcasts, and multimedia materials. Early implementations show 80-90% accuracy for clear audio, opening vast unstudied audiovisual archives.

Predictive acquisition AI: Beyond analyzing past circulation, AI will increasingly predict emerging research interests and community reading patterns. Imagine AI that monitors academic publication trends, social media discussions, curriculum changes, and demographic shifts to recommend acquisitions months before user demand becomes apparent.

Conversational AI reference: Current chatbots handle routine questions. Next-generation AI will support complex reference interviews — understanding nuanced information needs, conducting multi-step research strategies, and providing genuinely helpful research consultation at scale. This doesn't replace librarians but extends their reach to 24/7 availability.

Automated preservation AI: Digital preservation will shift from reactive to predictive — AI monitoring the format ecosystem, detecting obsolescence risks before they become critical, automatically triggering migration workflows, and maintaining digital collection integrity with minimal human intervention.

The arc of library AI is clear: automation of routine tasks, augmentation of professional judgment, and unlocking of inaccessible collections. Libraries that embrace these tools thoughtfully — with attention to privacy, equity, and professional values — will thrive. Those that resist will struggle with unsustainable operational burdens and declining relevance.
