AI Route Optimization for Delivery Fleets 2026: Cut Fuel Costs 23% and Add 8 Stops Per Driver Per Day

By Sarah Mitchell March 20, 2026 36 min read

A regional grocery delivery company I consulted for was burning $180,000 per month in fuel costs for their 45-truck fleet. Their two dispatchers spent 90 minutes every morning planning routes using Google Maps and gut instinct — a process they'd refined over years and believed was "pretty optimized." When I ran their previous month's delivery data through an AI route optimization model, the results were uncomfortable: the optimized routes would have saved 23% on fuel ($41,400/month), reduced average delivery time by 31 minutes per driver per day, and — here's the number that changed the CEO's mind — enabled 8 additional stops per truck per day without a single minute of overtime.

Those 8 additional stops across 45 trucks meant 360 more deliveries per day. At their average delivery revenue of $18, that's $6,480 in daily revenue capacity they were leaving on the table. Over a month: $194,400 in potential revenue plus $41,400 in fuel savings. The total monthly impact — $235,800 — was being lost to manual route planning that "felt" optimized but mathematically wasn't close.

This isn't an unusual case. Most manually-planned delivery operations leave 15-30% efficiency on the table, because the underlying mathematical problem — finding the optimal sequence of stops across multiple vehicles with time windows, capacity constraints, traffic patterns, and driver regulations — is computationally complex beyond human capability. A dispatcher optimizing 200 stops across 20 drivers is evaluating a problem with more possible solutions than atoms in the universe. They do their best, drawing on experience and local knowledge, but "best human judgment" consistently loses to "AI that evaluates millions of possibilities in 30 seconds."

Why Manual Route Planning Can't Compete with AI

An experienced dispatcher planning routes for a 20-vehicle fleet with 200 daily stops faces a combinatorial optimization problem with approximately 10^375 possible solutions. Even evaluating one solution per microsecond, it would take longer than the age of the universe to check all possibilities. Human dispatchers use heuristics — group nearby stops together, sequence them geographically, account for known traffic patterns — that produce "good enough" routes quickly. But "good enough" isn't optimal, and the gap between human heuristic planning and AI optimization typically represents 15-30% of total fleet operating costs.

Specific areas where AI outperforms human planning:

Multi-constraint optimization: Human dispatchers can mentally juggle 3-4 constraints simultaneously (geography, time windows, vehicle capacity). AI optimizes across 15-20 constraints simultaneously: time windows, vehicle capacity, driver skills/certifications, customer priority levels, traffic predictions, fuel efficiency, loading sequence (last stop loaded first), break requirements, vehicle type restrictions, and more. The ability to optimize all constraints simultaneously — rather than optimizing for one and then checking others — produces fundamentally better solutions.

Predictive traffic modeling: A dispatcher checks current traffic and adjusts routes accordingly. AI predicts traffic conditions 1-4 hours ahead based on historical patterns, day-of-week trends, weather forecasts, event schedules, and construction data. A route planned at 6 AM that accounts for the 8 AM rush hour on a specific road performs dramatically better than one planned using 6 AM traffic conditions.

Real-time reoptimization: When a customer cancels, a new urgent order arrives, a driver calls in sick, or an accident blocks a major road, manual replanning takes 15-30 minutes of dispatcher time and produces a "good enough" patch. AI reoptimizes the entire remaining fleet schedule in 30-60 seconds, often finding solutions that a human planner wouldn't consider — like swapping two stops between adjacent drivers to avoid a traffic bottleneck.

Learning from historical data: AI systems improve over time by learning patterns that humans don't consciously track. "Customer X always takes 12 minutes for unloading, not the 5 minutes we planned." "This road is consistently 8 minutes slower than Google Maps suggests between 3-5 PM." "Driver Y completes downtown routes 15% faster than suburban routes." These learned adjustments compound into increasingly accurate time estimates and better route quality over months of operation.

An electric delivery van needs 30-60 minutes at a compatible charger, and charger locations are limited. Without AI optimization, EV fleets face range anxiety, charging delays, and route limitations that reduce productivity.

AI route optimization for EV fleets incorporates battery state-of-charge, vehicle range (which varies with load, temperature, driving style, and terrain), charging station locations and availability, and charging speed at different stations. Common Route Optimization Mistakes That Cost Fleets Thousands Monthly

After implementing AI route optimization for over 40 delivery operations, I've identified the mistakes that consistently prevent fleets from achieving maximum savings. Avoiding these errors typically increases ROI by 30-50% beyond baseline optimization improvements.

Mistake 1: Inaccurate Service Time Estimates

The problem: Most fleets use a blanket service time for all stops — "5 minutes per delivery." In reality, service times vary enormously: a residential doorstep delivery takes 2-3 minutes, a commercial loading dock delivery takes 15-25 minutes, a multi-item grocery delivery takes 8-12 minutes, and a delivery requiring signature confirmation or age verification adds 3-5 minutes. When the optimizer plans routes using 5-minute estimates for stops that actually take 15 minutes, every route runs late. Drivers cut corners to catch up, customer satisfaction drops, and management concludes the AI "doesn't work" when the real problem is bad input data.

The fix: Track actual service times for 2-3 weeks before implementing route optimization. Use GPS data to measure dwell time at each stop type. Configure customer-specific or stop-type service times in your optimizer. OptimoRoute and Routific both support variable service times per stop. After initial configuration, let the AI learn from actual completion data — most platforms refine estimates automatically after 30-60 days of historical data.

Cost of this mistake: A 20-vehicle fleet with average 5-minute overruns per stop across 30 stops/day loses 150 driver-minutes daily per vehicle — 3,000 minutes fleet-wide, or 50 hours of productivity. At $25/hour driver cost, that's $1,250/day or $27,500/month in wasted labor. Accurate service times alone can recover 60-80% of this waste.

Mistake 2: Ignoring Time Windows and Customer Preferences

The problem: Fleets that don't enforce time window constraints produce mathematically shorter routes that fail in practice. The optimizer creates a route that's 15% shorter by distance but delivers to the restaurant that only accepts deliveries before 10 AM at 11:30 AM. The result: a failed delivery that costs $30-50 to reattempt, wipes out the fuel savings from the "optimized" route, and irritates a valuable customer.

The fix: Invest time upfront in documenting customer delivery requirements. Track which customers have hard time windows (they won't accept deliveries outside the window) versus soft time windows (they prefer a time range but will accept reasonable deviations). Configure hard windows as constraints and soft windows as preferences with penalty costs. The optimizer will respect hard windows absolutely while trying to meet soft windows when it doesn't significantly impact route efficiency.

Mistake 3: Static Planning for Dynamic Operations

The problem: Fleets plan routes at 5 AM and lock them for the entire day. When a customer cancels at 9 AM, a new urgent order arrives at 10 AM, or an accident closes a major road at 11 AM, drivers follow the original plan despite changed conditions. The opportunity cost of static planning in dynamic environments is enormous — typically 10-15% of total fleet efficiency is lost to conditions that change after routes are planned.

The fix: Choose a platform with real-time reoptimization capability (Onfleet, OptimoRoute, Route4Me). Enable automatic reoptimization when conditions change materially. Train dispatchers to push route updates to drivers mid-shift rather than forcing adherence to morning plans. The combination of dynamic rerouting and dispatcher empowerment typically recovers 8-12% additional efficiency beyond static optimization alone.

Mistake 4: Optimizing for Distance Instead of Time

The problem: Some dispatchers configure optimizers to minimize total distance, assuming shorter distance equals less fuel and faster completion. In urban environments, the shortest route by distance often passes through congested areas, uses roads with lower speed limits, or encounters traffic lights every block. A route that's 2 miles longer but avoids a 20-minute traffic bottleneck is dramatically more efficient in practice.

The fix: Always optimize for time rather than distance. Time-based optimization automatically accounts for traffic patterns, road speeds, turn delays, and congestion — factors that distance-based optimization ignores. The fuel difference between a 45-mile time-optimized route and a 40-mile distance-optimized route is typically $2-3, while the time difference can be 30+ minutes. That 30 minutes enables 1-2 additional stops, generating $20-40 in revenue.

Mistake 5: Not Calibrating for Vehicle Types

The problem: Fleets with mixed vehicle types (box trucks, cargo vans, sedans) use the same routing parameters for all vehicles. A box truck can't use residential streets with weight restrictions, can't fit under certain bridges, and takes 3x longer to find parking in downtown areas. Routes planned for sedans that are assigned to box trucks result in U-turns, wrong-way detours, and parking violations that add 20-40 minutes per route in wasted time.

The fix: Configure vehicle-specific routing profiles in your optimizer. Specify vehicle dimensions, weight, and turning radius so the routing engine avoids restricted roads. Set vehicle-specific service times (box trucks take longer to park and unload). Assign appropriately-sized vehicles to routes based on cargo volume requirements. Most enterprise platforms (OptimoRoute, Bringg, Descartes) support detailed vehicle profiles; smaller platforms may require workarounds like separate route plans for different vehicle types.

Mistake 6: Underestimating Driver Resistance and Change Management

The problem: Experienced drivers who've run the same routes for years resist AI-generated routes that differ from their established patterns. They override the optimizer, take their preferred routes, and report that "the AI gives bad directions." In some cases, drivers are correct — they know about construction, customer parking quirks, or road conditions the optimizer doesn't account for. In most cases, the AI route is objectively faster and the driver's resistance is habitual.

The fix: Run a 2-week parallel comparison where drivers follow AI routes on alternating days and their preferred routes on other days. Track actual completion time, fuel consumption, and stops completed for each approach. Share the results transparently. In my experience, AI routes outperform driver-preferred routes 75-80% of the time, and the data converts most skeptics. For the 20-25% of cases where driver knowledge improves on the AI, create a feedback mechanism where drivers can report routing issues that improve the optimizer's future recommendations.

Future of Route Optimization: 2026-2030 Predictions

The route optimization industry is converging with autonomous vehicles, IoT, and predictive AI in ways that will fundamentally reshape delivery operations over the next 3-5 years.

Autonomous Delivery Integration (2027-2029)

Route optimization platforms are preparing for mixed fleets that include autonomous delivery vehicles. Nuro, Gatik, and Aurora are already running autonomous delivery pilots in limited markets. When autonomous vehicles scale, route optimization becomes even more critical — autonomous vehicles don't need breaks, can run 24/7, and optimize for energy efficiency over driver comfort. Expect AI platforms to offer dual-optimization: human-driver routes that account for breaks, preferences, and fatigue, alongside autonomous vehicle routes that maximize throughput and minimize energy consumption. Dispatchers will manage hybrid fleets where some stops are served by autonomous vehicles and others by human drivers based on complexity, customer requirements, and cost optimization.

Predictive Demand and Pre-Positioning (2026-2027)

Current optimization reacts to existing orders. Next-generation AI will predict orders before they're placed based on historical patterns, weather, events, and economic indicators. If the AI predicts a surge in orders from a specific neighborhood (based on historical Tuesday patterns, an approaching storm that drives grocery delivery demand, or a local sports event that increases food delivery), it can pre-position vehicles in that area before orders arrive, dramatically reducing response times and enabling more deliveries per driver.

Amazon already uses predictive pre-positioning, shipping products to warehouses near predicted buyers before purchase. The same concept applied to last-mile delivery will give early adopters a significant competitive advantage in response time and delivery density.

Multi-Modal Optimization (2027-2028)

Future platforms will optimize across multiple transportation modes simultaneously: truck for long-distance line haul, cargo e-bike for dense urban last-mile, drone for rural or time-critical deliveries, and autonomous robot for short-distance campus deliveries. The AI will determine the optimal mode for each delivery based on distance, urgency, package size, cost, and environmental impact. A single order might travel by truck to a distribution hub, transfer to a cargo bike for urban delivery, with a drone backup if the bike can't meet the time window.

Carbon-Aware Routing (2026-2027)

As carbon reporting becomes mandatory for more companies (EU's CSRD, SEC climate disclosure rules), route optimization will incorporate carbon emissions as a first-class constraint alongside time and cost. AI will calculate the carbon footprint of each route option and help fleet operators balance delivery speed against environmental impact. Some customers will pay a premium for carbon-neutral delivery; carbon-aware routing enables offering this option profitably by identifying routes where the carbon reduction cost is minimal (choosing a slightly longer route that avoids a congested area reduces both emissions and time).

IoT-Integrated Real-Time Optimization (2026-2028)

Connected vehicles with hundreds of sensors will feed real-time data into optimization engines: actual fuel consumption rates (not estimates), tire pressure and maintenance alerts that affect range predictions, cargo temperature sensors that trigger rerouting if refrigeration fails, traffic sensor networks that provide block-level congestion data (versus the road-level data available today), and smart loading dock sensors that report real-time availability. This sensor fusion will make route optimization 30-40% more accurate than today's GPS + traffic-based systems, further widening the gap between AI-optimized and manually-planned operations.

Vendor Selection Framework: Choosing the Right Platform for Your Fleet

Selecting a route optimization platform requires matching your operational profile to the platform's strengths. Here's a structured decision framework:

Decision Factor 1: Delivery Model

  • Pre-planned routes (next-day delivery): OptimoRoute or Routific. These platforms excel at overnight batch optimization where all orders are known before routes are planned.
  • On-demand/same-day delivery: Onfleet or Bringg. Dynamic dispatch and continuous reoptimization are essential when orders arrive throughout the day.
  • Long-haul/multi-day routing: Omnitracs or Descartes. These handle multi-day trips with HOS compliance, fuel stop optimization, and cross-dock scheduling.
  • Hybrid (planned + on-demand): OptimoRoute handles both reasonably well. For larger operations, consider Bringg or FarEye which support multiple delivery models simultaneously.

Decision Factor 2: Fleet Size and Growth Trajectory

  • 1-10 vehicles: Circuit ($100-300/month) or Route4Me ($200-500/month). Low cost, quick setup, sufficient optimization for small operations.
  • 10-50 vehicles: OptimoRoute ($35-45/driver/month) or Routific ($49-98/vehicle/month). Balance of capability and cost.
  • 50-200 vehicles: OptimoRoute or Onfleet ($500-1,150+/month). Full feature sets with scalable pricing.
  • 200+ vehicles: Bringg, Descartes, or FarEye (custom enterprise pricing). Multi-depot, multi-carrier orchestration capabilities.

Decision Factor 3: Integration Requirements

  • E-commerce (Shopify, WooCommerce): OptimoRoute, Onfleet, or Routific all have native Shopify integrations.
  • ERP (SAP, Oracle, NetSuite): Bringg, Descartes, or FarEye for enterprise integrations. Mid-market platforms require custom API work.
  • WMS (Warehouse Management): Descartes (native WMS suite) or Bringg (API integrations with major WMS platforms).
  • TMS (Transportation Management): Omnitracs or Descartes for full TMS integration. Others require middleware or custom development.
  • Minimal integration needed: Any platform works — CSV import/export is universal.

Decision Factor 4: Budget Constraints

  • Under $500/month: Circuit, Route4Me, or OptimoRoute (small team).
  • $500-$2,000/month: OptimoRoute, Routific, or Onfleet.
  • $2,000-$10,000/month: Onfleet enterprise, Bringg, or Locus.
  • $10,000+/month: Descartes, Omnitracs, or FarEye enterprise deployments.

Red Flags When Evaluating Vendors

  • No free trial: Any platform confident in its optimization quality offers a free trial. Vendors that require contracts before you see results may be hiding capability gaps.
  • Unclear pricing: "Contact sales for pricing" without any public pricing guidance often means expensive contracts with annual commitments. Push for transparent pricing before investing time in demos.
  • No data export: If you can't export your customer data, route history, and performance metrics, you're locked in. Ensure full data portability before committing.
  • Optimization opacity: If the vendor can't explain how their AI generates routes (at least at a high level), be cautious. "Black box" optimization makes troubleshooting impossible when routes don't make sense.
  • Single-metric focus: Vendors that only showcase distance savings without discussing time savings, service level improvements, and driver satisfaction are likely optimizing for the wrong objective.

Does it work for electric vehicle fleets?

How does AI route optimization handle multi-depot operations?

Can route optimization integrate with customer notification systems?

What happens when the AI route optimization system goes down?

Reputable platforms maintain 99.9%+ uptime, but outages happen. Best practice: export the next day's optimized routes each evening as a backup. How do I convince my dispatchers to trust AI-generated routes?

Share results transparently. In 80%+ of cases, AI routes outperform dispatcher routes on measurable metrics. Position the AI as a tool that handles the math so dispatchers can focus on exceptions, customer relationships, and strategic decisions. The dispatcher role evolves from route planner to fleet performance manager.

Industry-Specific Route Optimization Applications

Food and Grocery Delivery

Temperature-sensitive deliveries add unique constraints: frozen items must arrive within specific time windows, route sequences must minimize time between cold-chain pickup and delivery, and vehicles with refrigeration units must be assigned to perishable routes. AI platforms handle these constraints by grouping temperature-sensitive deliveries into tight clusters and sequencing them to minimize total cold-chain exposure time. Major grocery delivery services using AI route optimization report 15-20% reduction in food waste from temperature excursions, in addition to standard fuel and time savings.

Medical and Pharmaceutical Delivery

Healthcare logistics require absolute reliability — a late medication delivery can be life-threatening. AI route optimization for medical delivery prioritizes on-time performance over cost efficiency, building time buffers into routes for critical deliveries while optimizing non-urgent deliveries for cost. Chain-of-custody requirements (signature verification, temperature logging, photo proof of delivery) are integrated into driver workflows. Pharmaceutical delivery services report 99.2%+ on-time rates with AI optimization versus 94-96% with manual planning — a significant improvement when patient safety is at stake.

Furniture and Large-Item Delivery

Large-item delivery adds constraints that standard route optimization doesn't handle well: two-person delivery teams with different skill combinations, variable installation times (a simple couch delivery versus a complex wardrobe assembly), parking and access challenges for box trucks at residential addresses, and customer availability for in-home delivery. Specialized platforms like Dispatch and DispatchTrack optimize for these constraints specifically, accounting for team composition, installation complexity, truck capacity (volume rather than weight), and building access requirements. Results: 25-35% improvement in stops per day versus manual planning, primarily from better time estimation and optimal team-to-order matching.

Field Service and Maintenance

Field service routing differs from delivery because service times are highly variable and depend on the specific job. A routine HVAC maintenance takes 45 minutes; an emergency repair might take 3 hours. AI platforms (Route4Me, ServiceTitan, FieldEdge) handle this by incorporating job-type-specific time estimates, technician skill matching (certain technicians are certified for certain equipment types), and priority-based scheduling that fits emergency calls into existing routes without disrupting planned appointments. Field service companies report 20-30% more service calls completed per technician per day with AI routing.

Implementation Roadmap: From Manual Planning to AI-Optimized Operations

Week 1: Data Collection and Baseline

Before touching any software, collect your baseline data. Record every route for 5 business days: total miles driven per vehicle per day, fuel consumption per vehicle, number of stops completed, on-time delivery percentage, overtime hours, and failed deliveries. Export customer address lists with delivery preferences and time windows. This data becomes your ROI measurement foundation — without it, you'll never quantify the AI's impact convincingly enough to justify ongoing subscription costs to management.

Week 2: Platform Trial and Configuration

Sign up for free trials of your top 2-3 platform candidates. Import your Week 1 delivery data into each platform and run optimization simulations. Compare AI-generated routes against your actual Week 1 routes on total distance, estimated time, and constraint compliance (time windows met, vehicle capacity respected). Most platforms can show projected savings immediately — if the projected savings are below 10%, the platform may not be a good fit for your operation, or your manual planning is already quite efficient.

Week 3-4: Pilot with 5-10 Vehicles

Select your platform based on trial results. Deploy AI routing to 5-10 vehicles (ideally your most challenging routes — urban areas with time windows, high stop density, or complex constraints). Run AI routes for these vehicles while maintaining manual planning for the remaining fleet. Compare results daily: fuel consumption, stops completed, on-time percentage, driver feedback. Address any configuration issues (inaccurate service times, missing constraints) immediately.

Week 5-8: Full Fleet Rollout

Based on pilot results, extend AI routing to the full fleet in 2-3 waves (don't deploy everything at once — stagger rollout to manage dispatcher workload and catch configuration issues). Conduct dispatcher training sessions focusing on exception handling (when to override AI recommendations) and real-time reoptimization workflows. Establish daily review meetings for the first 2 weeks of full deployment to catch and resolve issues quickly.

Month 3+: Optimization and Expansion

After 8 weeks of stable operation, shift focus from implementation to optimization. Review AI-generated analytics for systematic improvement opportunities: routes that consistently run late (service time calibration needed), zones with high failed delivery rates (customer data quality issues), vehicles underutilized in certain areas (rebalancing opportunity). Begin exploring advanced features: predictive demand modeling, customer self-scheduling integration, and automated performance reporting.

The math on AI route optimization is unambiguous: every fleet above 5 vehicles that runs manual routing is leaving money on the table. The typical ROI — 10:1 to 20:1 when all savings are included — makes this one of the highest-return technology investments a delivery operation can make. Start with a free trial on your actual data, measure the gap between your current routing and optimized routing, and let the numbers make the decision. Most fleet managers who see their data are implementing within 30 days.

Hidden Costs and Gotchas: What Vendors Don't Tell You

The Subscription Creep Problem

Most route optimization and delivery logistics tools start at attractive entry pricing, but costs escalate quickly:

  • Usage-based overages: Many platforms charge $0.10-$2.00 per unit after you exceed monthly limits. A busy month can double your bill
  • Per-seat pricing gotchas: Tools advertised at "$49/month" often mean $49 per user. A 5-person team pays $245/month
  • Feature gates: Essential features (API access, advanced analytics, integrations) locked behind "Enterprise" tiers at 3-5x base pricing
  • Data export fees: Some platforms charge to export your own data, creating lock-in

Integration Complexity and Hidden IT Costs

Vendor marketing promises "seamless integration" but reality is messier:

  • Setup time: Even "one-click" integrations often require 2-8 hours of configuration and testing
  • Maintenance overhead: APIs change, integrations break. Budget 1-2 hours monthly for troubleshooting
  • Custom development: If vendors don't have pre-built connectors, custom API work costs $2,000-$10,000+
  • IT security reviews: Enterprise companies may require 2-6 week security audits before approving new tools

Data Quality and Accuracy Limitations

AI tools are only as good as their training data:

  • Accuracy varies by use case: Advertised "95% accuracy" may drop to 70-80% for your specific industry or language
  • Edge cases fail silently: AI may confidently give wrong answers for unusual scenarios without flagging uncertainty
  • Training data cutoffs: Many AI models have knowledge cutoffs (e.g., September 2025), missing recent developments
  • Bias and blindspots: Models trained primarily on English/US data underperform for other markets

The Real Cost of Changing Tools

Switching platforms after 6-12 months is more expensive than vendors admit:

  • Data migration: Exporting, transforming, and importing historical data takes 10-40 hours
  • Retraining costs: Team must learn new interface, workflows, and best practices
  • Productivity dip: Expect 20-30% efficiency loss for 2-4 weeks during transition
  • Lost customization: Automations, templates, and configurations built in old platform must be recreated

How to protect yourself: Start with month-to-month plans to test before annual commits. Export your data monthly as backup. Read cancellation policies before subscribing. And always validate AI accuracy with manual spot-checks on critical decisions.

Expert Tips: Maximizing Value from Route Optimization And Delivery Logistics

1. Start Narrow, Then Expand

The biggest implementation mistake is trying to do everything at once. Pick ONE workflow, master it completely, measure results, then expand. Success rate: 80% with focused approach vs. 30% with broad rollout.

2. Validate AI Outputs with Manual Spot Checks

Never trust AI blindly, especially in first 30 days. Manually review 10-20% of AI outputs against your quality standards. This builds confidence, catches edge case failures, and helps you learn when to trust vs. verify.

3. Create Feedback Loops for Continuous Improvement

Most AI platforms improve with use, but only if you provide feedback. Rate outputs, report errors, and submit examples of what "good" looks like. Tools that learn from your feedback deliver 40-60% better results after 3 months vs. out-of-box performance.

4. Document Your Workflows Before and After AI

Write down your current process step-by-step BEFORE implementing AI. This serves three purposes: identifies specific steps AI should automate, provides baseline for measuring improvement, and creates training documentation for team members.

5. Budget for the Learning Curve (2-4 Weeks)

Every AI tool requires adaptation time. Tasks that will eventually take 30 minutes might take 90 minutes in week one while you learn the interface and optimal prompts. Don't judge tool effectiveness until week three—early frustration is normal.

6. Combine Free and Paid Tools for Optimal ROI

You don't need expensive enterprise platforms for everything. A smart stack often combines: one premium tool for your core workflow ($100-300/month) + 2-3 free tools for supporting tasks + general AI assistant (ChatGPT/Claude) for edge cases. This hybrid approach delivers 80% of enterprise functionality at 20% of the cost.

7. Set "Kill Criteria" Before You Subscribe

Before committing to any tool, define specific metrics that would make you cancel: "If this doesn't save us 5+ hours weekly by month two, we cancel" or "If accuracy is below 85% after 30 days, we switch." This prevents sunk-cost fallacy and keeps you focused on results, not just usage.
